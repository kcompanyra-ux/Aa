The Practice

Shinrin-yoku,
spoken in cedar.

Forest bathing is not hiking. It is the deliberate act of being unhurried among trees — letting the canopy filter the light and, with it, whatever you carried through the gate. The Japanese call it shinrin-yoku. Our guides call it Tuesday.

Stillwood has been left largely alone since 1987. The trails were laid by hand, the creek was never dammed, and the loudest thing you will hear all morning is a varied thrush working the far ridge.

01

Arrive

Leave the phone in the cedar locker at the gate. The first ten minutes of every walk are silent — long enough for your pulse to find the forest's tempo.

02

Slow

Guides walk at roughly one kilometre an hour. Speed is the only thing we ask you to give up. Most walkers stop checking the time by the second bend.

03

Notice

Five senses, one at a time. Bark, then birdsong, then the smell of rain that fell three days ago. Most people say the light through the mist is what they carry home.

Before you ask

Quiet questions.

A slow, sensory walk with no destination pressure — the practice of shinrin-yoku, developed in Japan in the 1980s. Studies link it to lower cortisol and blood pressure. We link it to noticing things you'd otherwise miss.

No. The slowest walker sets the pace, and Gentle trails are wheelchair-friendly for their first kilometre. If you can walk to a kettle, you can walk with us.

We walk in rain — it's the forest's best voice, and the moss turns almost luminous. Ponchos wait at the gate. Only lightning closes the trails, and then we reschedule you free of charge.

Walkers aged 8 and up are welcome on Gentle trails, and they're usually the best noticers in the group. The Dawn session is adults-only — it's the quietest hour we have.

Leashed dogs are welcome on the Fern Hollow Loop before 10:00, when the ground-nesting birds are least disturbed. Everywhere else, the forest belongs to its residents.
